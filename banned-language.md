## Core Principle

If a reader could guess AI wrote it, rewrite it. Clean writing has a human behind it:
opinions, rhythm, mess, first-person perspective. Sterile and voiceless is as bad as
slop.

**Always first person, never third person.** This is my own dissertation, written from inside the practice it describes. A self-reference in prose, a caption, or a table cell is "I," "my," or "me" — never "Darren," "the author," "the researcher," or any other third-person stand-in.

## Hard Rules


Non-negotiable. No exceptions.


- No em dashes. Use periods, commas, colons, or restructure.
- No passive construction that hides who decided. "The threshold was set to 0.45" hides me; "I set the threshold to 0.45" does not. A passive whose agent is named, or whose agent does not matter ("the tune was published in Boston in 1863"), is allowed, and a paragraph built entirely from subject-verb-object sentences is its own failure (see Biodiversity).
- No synonym cycling. Pick one word for a concept and use it throughout.
- No curly quotes. Use straight quotes ("...").
- Stay informed of new AI writing tropes as they emerge and avoid them on sight — this list isn't closed

---

## Principles


Guidelines, not absolutes. Use judgment.


- Mix sentence lengths deliberately. Let some run long. Then stop. Land a line.
- Two items beat three. One beats two when it's strong enough.
- Avoid excessive exclamation points.
- Declarative sentences, aggressive length variation, concrete nouns over abstractions, strong verbs over adverbs. Open with substance, not a hook.
- No staccato fragmentation in long-form. [long-form only]
- Vary sentence-opening grammar and vocabulary deliberately across a paragraph — if a rewrite for one of the above rules produces the same fix pattern twice in a row (e.g., two consecutive sentences starting "This dissertation..." or two uses of "states" in the same paragraph), find a different construction rather than repeating the nearest fix.

The problem is not all -ly words. "Barely," "nearly," "only," "quickly" are fine. The

**Offenders:** really, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply, truly,
fundamentally, inherently, inevitably, interestingly, importantly, crucially.

If the verb needs an adverb to land, the verb is wrong. Pick a stronger one.

## Banned Sentence Patterns

**No reversal constructions**: "It's not X, it's Y," "Not as X — but as Y."


**"Not X, it's Y" constructions:**
- "That's not X. That's Y."
- "It's not about X, it's about Y"
- "X isn't just A. It's B."
- "Not only X, but also Y"
- "The X, not a Y..." (e.g., "The event, not a report filed afterward.")

**"X rather than Y" constructions:**
- "...described briefly below rather than walked through."
The pull toward "rather than" is the same reflex as "not X, it's Y": defining something by what it isn't instead of just stating what it is. If a sentence has an urge to attach a negative half, cut the negative half and see if the sentence still says everything it needs to.


**Template structures:**
- "The result? [Statement]."
- "Here's the thing:" / "But here's the kicker:"
- "The question is:" / "The answer? [Statement]."


State the point directly instead.


**"X, and it is Y" tacked-on definition:** "The joint that this whole recipe turns on sits right here, and it is the part I am still tuning." A clause bolted on with "and it is" to redefine or re-emphasize what was just said, instead of committing to one sentence. Fix: pick one half. Either state the claim once, or split into two real sentences that each add something new.


**Vague declaratives:**
- "The reasons are structural."
- "The implications are significant."
- "The stakes are high."


If a sentence says something is important without showing the specific thing, cut it.

## Banned Structural Patterns


**False Agency:** Don't let objects perform human actions. "A complaint becomes a fix"
should be "The team fixed it." Name the human doing the thing.

**No "carry" for inanimate things or ideas**: "because the free path would not carry it," "the alarm carries the weight of every touch that came without asking." Same failure as false agency above, but specific enough to name on its own: a field, a path, a sound, an alarm cannot "carry" weight or meaning, only a person can. Fix: cut it. "Because the free path would not carry it" loses nothing by dropping the clause entirely. "The alarm carries the weight of every touch that came without asking" needs a human subject and a real claim, not a personified alarm.


**Narrator-from-a-Distance:** "Nobody designed this." / "People tend to..." Put the
reader in the room. Use "you."


**Dramatic Fragmentation [long-form only]:** "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]."
Write complete sentences in long-form copy.


**Negative Listing:** "Not a X... Not a Y... A Z." Just state Z directly.


**Rhetorical Setups:** "What if [reframe]?" / "Think about it:" Make the point.


**Fake Personal Voice:** Using "I" without committing to an actual opinion. "I think this
could be interesting for some people" is not a personal voice. "This works" is. Say the
thing.

**Pattern-Dodging:** Banning "tapestry" doesn't ban "rich mosaic of ideas." The
underlying pattern is using decorative metaphors to describe abstract concepts without
adding meaning. Ban the pattern, not just the word.

**No meta-commentary**: "In this section," "As a researcher," references to the act of writing itself.

**No therapeutic language**: "I hear you," "sit with this," "hold space for.

## Grammar Rules

**No symmetry padding**: artificial three-part lists, sentences that exist only to balance a prior sentence rather than say something new.

**Active voice with named humans where a decision is at stake.** A sentence that reports a choice names the person who made it. No inanimate objects performing human actions. Outside that, sentence subjects may be devices doing their literal operation, materials, events, clauses, or places; the "I" opener is one option among many.


**Copula substitutes.** Avoid elaborate constructions that replace simple is/are/has.
Watch for: serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features, offers. Use the
simple verb.


**No padding with three.** Don't force a third item to seem comprehensive. If you only
have two real things, use two. Three padded examples are worse than two sharp ones.


**No synonym cycling.** Pick one term and stick with it. AI has repetition-penalty code
that causes excessive synonym substitution.


**No superficial -ing tacking.** Don't attach present participle phrases for fake depth.
Watch for: highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing,
showcasing.


**No Wh- sentence starters.** Don't open paragraphs with What, When, Where, Why, Who.
Forces stronger openings.

**No "room" as scene-setting or metaphor**: "in the room," "in a room," "leaving that room with," "put the two in the same room," or any other construction that reaches for a literal or figurative room to stage a moment. Say what actually happened instead — who was present, what was said, what changed — without the room as a prop. This applies even when a room is literally involved; find another way to say it.

**No retrospective-completion citations or concepts**: "Baraka had already stated this movement's whole premise," "Bogost gives this dissertation's own method a name it did not yet have," "names what this section has been circling from its first paragraph," "Eshun also names what such a method is up against," "Engulfment names something more exact than the foreclosure described above," "Hayles's own vocabulary... names that asymmetry more precisely than abstraction ever did." The shared move: a source, or a concept/term already in play, is introduced as though it arrives to complete, name, or retroactively validate something the prose was already secretly building toward, rather than being cited or used for what it says. This isn't limited to theorists "naming" things — a concept, a piece of vocabulary, or a formula can do the same reflexive move ("X names Y precisely," "the term captures exactly what was described above"). Grammatically this usually shows up as "[source/concept] names/gives/states what [this work] has been [-ing]," "[source] had already [verb]ed," or "[source] supplies this decades/long before this dissertation had a vocabulary for it." Fix: state what the source or concept says and how it bears on the argument, without staging the citation as a reveal. Plain verbs work fine here: "states," "describes," "is."

**No "long before" temporal-priority construction**: "bodies confined, suspended, and moved to someone else's optimum, long before a machine ever calculated one." Runs the same move as the rule above but on a timeline instead of a citation — a human or historical reality is staged as preceding a technical one, as though the sequence itself does the arguing. Fix: state the connection directly instead of leaning on "before"/"long before" to imply it. Short declarative sentences work better here than a dependent clause reaching backward in time.

**No "it's X before Y," in any variation**: 'Before' is banned when it implies priority or causation. Plain chronology stays. "it's where the word got manufactured before spreading into the movements," "It is the argument the algorithm was making before this project touched it," "that is the substrate box... specified before any of them existed," "it was a refusal before it was a design," "the drone is a condition before it is a sound." Two moves fused into one sentence: an identifying frame ("it's," "it is," "it was," "this is," "that is," "there is") that designates something as the origin, essence, or site of a process, and a "before" clause that then tracks its motion outward or its conversion into something else. Both halves assert rather than argue, and the "before" smuggles in priority or causation the sentence has not earned. Same family as the "long before" rule above, different scaffolding. Includes the bare essence version, "X is really Y before it is Z," which pretends to reveal a hidden order of being. Fix: break it into two declaratives and make the relationship a claim someone could disagree with. "It's where the word got manufactured before spreading into the movements" becomes "The word was manufactured in this file. It spread from here into the movements." If the causal link is the point, give it its own sentence and defend it. **Plain chronology is unaffected**: "small pieces of it breaking off into my palm before I even dropped it" is a sequence of events in a narrative, not an argument wearing a timeline.

**No bare "before" sequencing to smuggle in an argument**: "My hand goes up before I have decided anything," "It changes your relationship to a tool before you've touched it once." Same family as the two rules above, but without the identifying frame ("it's," "it was") or the word "long" -- just X [verb], before Y [verb], used to imply that X precedes, exceeds, or causes Y without saying so. Fix: cut the "before" clause and state the claim directly, or keep only the plain physical sequence if that's genuinely what's happening. "My hand goes up before I have decided anything" becomes "I tend to reach for my hair, preemptively, as if in anticipation of something desired." "It changes your relationship to a tool before you've touched it once" becomes "It changes your relationship to a tool."

**Transduction Verbs: name the operator** (reconciled with False Agency and Source-as-Actor). Do not hide a computational or physical process inside agentless phrasing. Banned: "is converted to/into," "gets converted," "is transformed into," "turns into," and "becomes" when describing a signal-chain step with no operator. 
**The "Operator Naming" Rule:** In lab reporting, a device cannot possess figurative intent ("the hydrophone forces," "the threshold shears"), but it does execute commands with absolute physical reality. Name what does the operation using strict DSP, architectural, or materials-science verbs:

*   *Signal & Control:* route, govern, dictate, attenuate, trigger, fire, isolate, extract, clamp, lock, sample, ingest.
*   *Architecture & Limit:* constrain, bound, restrict, abandon, deposit.
A device performing its actual operation is not false agency (e.g., "the ADC samples," "the band filter isolates," "the patch routes"). Where the design decision is the point, the subject is me: "I map," "I locked the threshold," "I severed the connection."
**Exception, narrow:** "permitted to become" stays. The permitter is part of the claim; that phrase is the thesis.

Academic Eshun: see VOICE-CARD.md. The default register is kinetic and argued.

**The "Years of Experience" Trope.** Do not offer duration as the credential: "after years of working with sensors," "a decade of practice has taught me," "as someone with years of experience." The credential is the specific modification: what I changed in the tool and what the change exposed. Write from the position of the person who opened the device.

**No Conceptual Hedging.** Banned on claims about the system, the sources, or the argument: "of sorts," "in some fashion," "perhaps," "it seems," "seemingly," "arguably," "to some extent," "somewhat." If a metric is the chemical memory of the water, state that it is. If a prototype works, say it works; if it failed, say it failed and what failed. **Exception:** a first-person report of uncertain self-observation is a fact and stays. "Zoned-out, well I don't really know, I can't seem to recall exactly what the motion is" (What Good Looks Like, above) reports what I could and could not observe about my own body. Keep that register where the uncertainty is the observation.

**No Administrative Signposting.** Do not narrate the outline. Banned: "The next section explores X," "This section," "Three sections follow," "The first concerns," "I want to state my central claim here," "One claim organizes this paper," "I mention it because," "the consequence is the argument of this paper." If the preceding paragraph did its job, the next paragraph begins. A figure or table reference is not signposting: "(Table 1)" or "Table 1 lists" is fine. An abstract may say what the paper reports, because that is what an abstract is for. Same family as the Meta-Commentary rule above; this is its paragraph-to-paragraph form.

If a sentence sounds like it belongs on a motivational poster, rewrite it. Pull-quote
energy is AI energy.

## The Tests


**Specificity Test:** Does every sentence contain a concrete detail, a named person, or a
committed opinion? Vague declaratives fail this test. The fastest fix is a real number:
"$1,700 in taxes" beats "significant savings." "6 months" beats "several months." If you
can't add the specific detail, cut the sentence.


**Infomercial Test:** Read it aloud. Does it sound like something a trusted friend would
say, or a late-night infomercial? If you'd hear it on a commercial, rewrite it.

**Commitment Test:** Find every sentence with "I." Does it commit to an actual opinion,
or hedge toward one? "I think this might be worth considering for some use cases" is not
a committed opinion. Cut the hedge or drop the "I."

## Source-as-Actor

The single most pervasive tell in the draft. A cited person, or a concept, becomes the
grammatical subject of a physical verb, so the sentence feels like it made an argument
when all it did was stage one.

- "Don Ihde reaches for my own instrument to make the point."
- "Their framework leaves a seat open and declines to fill it."
- "Barad takes on Bennett's move and refuses it."
- "Spillers supplies the history Bennett's democratizing gesture has to pass through."
- "Kodwo Eshun found the same act in a different instrument."
- "Barad's apparatus fares no better left unhistoricized."
- "Grimshaw and Garner carry the claim past the instrument and into the ear."
- "Natalie Loveless turns the same pressure on institutional form."
- "Rayvon Fouché sorts such acts along a continuum."
- "Each lineage holds one piece of what this project needed."

Watch verbs: reaches, takes on, refuses, leaves, supplies, hands over, sorts, fares,
turns, places, found, carries past, holds one piece of.

Fix: the subject of the sentence is me, or the claim. State what the source says, with
its citation. Then take a separate sentence for what I do with it. A reader should be
able to state the source's position from the sentence that cites it.

## Resemblance as Argument

Asserting that two things look alike is not a finding, and in this draft it is the
default way a historical anecdote gets attached to the research.

- "they carry the same lesson at different scales"
- "The three cases in this section sit at three different depths on that same scale"
- "That argument has the same shape as Benjamin's technical fix"
- "the cryopod and the hold of a slave ship are the same vessel, told twice"
- "one operation, stated once by Moten and demonstrated four times across this score"
- "Those devices are drones in the sense just defined"

Fix: state what the resemblance obliges the work to do differently. If nothing follows
from it, cut the comparison and keep the citation.

## Pre-Absorbed Objection

A sentence whose only job is to concede or bracket something so the reader will not raise
it. Reads as flinching.

- "This dissertation does not resolve that disagreement, and does not need to."
- "That is a real cost, conceded here rather than argued away."
- "Other operations of the same kind almost certainly exist and are not catalogued here."
- "not a flaw in Bogost's theory waiting to be corrected from outside it"
- "The three cases in this section sit at three different depths on that same scale, not
  three unrelated anecdotes."

Fix: cut. A real objection gets its own paragraph and a real answer.

## The X-Waiting-To-Be-Y Construction

"a procedure waiting to be applied to something," "an unfinished argument waiting for a
winner," "a flaw waiting to be corrected." Fix: state what it is.

## Corpus Statistics as Evidence

"'Sonic' appears on four of *Persuasive Games*'s 463 pages." No reader believes a person
counted this, and it reads as machine output. Fix: quote the passage where the thing does
or does not happen and let the absence show.

## Appositive Gloss Stacking

Colon or comma followed by a compressed restatement that adds no new proposition,
repeated across consecutive sentences.

- "Extraction removes value or labor from a body or a landscape for use elsewhere: the
  labor the hold pulled from a commodity that could speak."
- "Abstraction distances what was taken from its material reality, reducing it to symbol,
  category, or code: cargo weight standing in for a body."
- "Computation reduces what abstraction has loosened into calculable, machine-legible
  units: a sensor threshold, a pixel array, a training label."

Three in a row, identical shape. This is the symmetry-padding rule operating at paragraph
scale. One gloss per paragraph maximum, and only when it supplies a concrete instance the
reader could not have supplied.

## Aphoristic Closers

A short declarative with the cadence of an earned conclusion and no new claim behind it.

- "The machine came later, but the desire for optimizing did not."
- "Quantification was the cutting mechanism."
- "Both are always true at once, the way a chord is always more than one note."
- "Theory here is not scaffolding erected before the building."
- "Neither device closes."

Chat rewards the punchy line because it closes a turn. A dissertation paragraph does not
close, it advances. One per section at most, and it has to be a claim someone could
dispute.

## Repeated Metaphor Quota

"[system] was not built to recognize / see [Black hair / Black skin]" and its variants.
Overused across the manuscript. One statement at full strength, in Movement III where it
belongs. Everywhere else, state the specific failure: the threshold, the training set,
the missing category.

## Clarification to an Existing Rule

The name-as-verb rule recommends **"states"** as a replacement. That holds when the
subject is a person. It fails when the subject is a concept: "this kind of resistance
states the mechanism" is the false-agency problem wearing a different verb. Use "states"
for people, not for ideas.

## The Consequence Test

Not a ban. The positive rule the bans above are protecting.

> Every historical or theoretical passage ends on something that changed in the making: a
> decision, a constraint, a threshold, a refusal, a name, an approach rejected. If that
> cannot be stated in one sentence, the passage is decoration. Cut it, or compress it to
> a citation.

In the Composer's Note, the passages that fail this test are exactly the passages that
read as 90% story and 10% research: Tudor and Cage at the Pearlstein, Baraka's
typewriter, Eastman and *Song Books*. The passages that pass it are the longest ones in
the introduction and read as necessary: Bennett against Barad against Spillers, and the
Hayles episode that produces the rename.

## 2026-09-18

### Unresolved-As-Method

Stating that a tension will be held rather than resolved, in place of saying what is missing and what this work supplies instead.

The pattern: a section identifies a real disagreement between two positions, declines to settle it, and presents the declining as a methodological virtue. Signals include "does not resolve," "holds rather than resolves," "the tension is held open," "this dissertation does not resolve X, and does not need to," "sequenced rather than harmonized," and any sentence whose work is to announce that a question stays open.

**Why it goes.** Per Darren, 2026-09-18: "we, in general, need to do away with the whole 'does not resolve' nonsense, that is not what a dissertation is for or does, in fact it is kind of antithetical." An undeclared refusal to conclude reads as an inability to conclude. What most of these passages are actually doing is ordinary scholarly work, identifying a gap, which has its own shape and does not need a licence.

**The fix.** Say what the two positions each fail to supply, and what this work supplies in their place. That is gap identification and it concludes. Where two commitments genuinely both operate, state what each one accounts for and move on, without narrating the decision not to choose.

**Do not borrow a scholar to license it.** Moten's refusal of closure, "an ongoing and reconstructive improvisation of ensemble," was quoted twice in the Composer's Note to justify this pattern. Per Darren: "the Moten point, while valid for him, is fine IF we actually use it, but I do not see a reason to." A passage may quote a scholar's own argument about non-closure when that argument is the subject. It may not use one as cover for declining to conclude.

**One exception, narrow.** An argument for non-resolution stated by a cited scholar, engaged as that scholar's claim, and answered.

**Distinct from an irreducible Core.** A chapter's Core may be compound, with parts that resist reduction to one sentence, and a future pass should not tidy one into a single line. That is a fact about how the argument is recorded. It carries no permission for prose that declines to conclude.

## 2026-09-22

From Darren's review of the rewritten Composer's Note.

### Refuse, in every form

**Banned: refuse, refuses, refused, refusing, refusal.** Per Darren: "I think 'refuse' needs to go on the banned list, it crops up far too much." Part of the reason it spread is that this file used to recommend "refuses to" as the replacement for "never." That recommendation is withdrawn.

It fails in two ways. As false agency it is the same problem as "carry": an encounter, a score, a framework or a lineage cannot refuse anything. And even with a real agent, it has become a reflex that makes every act of disagreement sound like a stand. When a person did decline something, say what they did: rejected it, would not use it, turned it down, wrote against it. Quoted sources keep their own words.

### The Appended Flourish

A sentence does its work in its first clause and then keeps going with a second clause that personifies an abstraction and adds nothing. Per Darren: "The whole [important actually useful part of the sentence], [totally useless part with bs personification]; is really annoying."

Examples from the draft, all flagged by Darren:

- "The two halves feed each other, **and the feeding is the framework.**"
- "Each movement foregrounds one domain **without setting the others down.**"
- "Their framework leaves a seat open **and declines to fill it.**"

Two close relatives, same fix:

- **The personified abstraction standing alone.** "None of the four has a fixed place in line." "Neither device closes." A list of four terms has no place in line; a device does not close. If the point is that the cuts are not a cycle, say that the cuts are not a cycle.
- **The closing gloss on a figure or a claim.** "That is a formal decision as much as a theoretical one." A sentence that tells the reader how to classify the sentence before it.

**Fix:** end the sentence where its claim ends. If the second clause was carrying a real point, make that point a sentence of its own with a real subject. The linter cannot see this pattern reliably. Check for it by eye: any sentence whose last clause gives an idea, a list, a framework or a device a body or an intention.

### Candidates, not yet banned

Darren has flagged these as irritants without ruling them out. Watch the count; do not lean on them.

- **build / built** as a reflexive verb for making a framework, a case, a claim or a drone ("the failure ST is built to avoid"). Literal building of instruments is fine.
- **"not designed around / not built to recognize"** as the stock phrase for computer vision and Black hair. Darren: "overused in the dissertation."

### 2026-09-30 Hard Bans 
- **No "bracket" or "bracketing" as a verb.** Do not use this to describe an omission or a theoretical framing device ("Grisey brackets culture out"). It pretends erasure is a neutral framing choice. Replace with verbs of active exclusion: strip, sever, filter, erase, abandon, or exclude.

## 2026-09-29: Biodiversity

Per Darren: generated prose "lacks grammatical and vocabulary depth and variety. Writing should be like a climate region or micro-ecosystem. It has its own flavor and style, but also biodiversity. Use more complex words and sentences. Not the rehash of the same old thing over and over."

The bans above remove weeds. They do not plant anything. Enforced alone, they push generated prose into one species of sentence: "I chose X. I map Y. The threshold does Z." Every sentence passes and the paragraph is a monoculture. This section is the positive rule.

**Sentence architecture.** Across any paragraph of five or more sentences, use at least three of these, and do not repeat one twice in a row:

- the periodic sentence, which withholds its main clause to the end ("Beneath the surface, wired through a JFET preamp I soldered myself, listens a hydrophone.")
- the cumulative sentence, a main clause extended by modifiers that each add a fact
- the subordinate opener ("Because," "Although," "Where," "Once," "Until")
- the prepositional or adverbial opener ("Across all three movements," "In the lab," "For now")
- inversion or object-fronting ("Earth, rock, and soil furnish the first movement")
- the semicolon pairing two independent clauses that answer each other
- the colon that delivers an enumeration or a payoff
- the appositive that identifies without a new sentence
- the parenthetical aside that supplies a fact
- the short declarative, used as punctuation after something long

**Openers and Agency (The Fronting Rule).** No two consecutive sentences open with the same word. No single opener (including "I" and "The") on more than a third of a paragraph's sentences. 
**To maintain active first-person agency without violating the "I" cap:** Decouple the grammatical subject from the sentence opener. Use object-fronting, absolute phrases, or subordinate clauses to delay the "I." 
*   *Failure (Monoculture):* "I built the hydrophone. I set the threshold. I mapped the output." 
*   *Success (Fronting):* "Wired through a custom JFET preamp, the hydrophone listens from within the water. Setting the threshold to 0.45 isolates the resonance. Once the sensor registers the attack, I route the data to the drone."

**Rhythm.** Sentence lengths in a paragraph should range widely: a four-word sentence and a forty-word sentence belong in the same paragraph more often than not.

**Vocabulary.** Reach for the exact word, including the less common one, when it is exact: friable, attenuate, incessant, accretion, jettison, delimit, potable, cadence, provenance, sediment, confer. A precise uncommon word is range; an ornamental one is the Pattern-Dodging failure above. The test is whether a specialist would use the word in that sentence.

**Terms of art stay fixed.** This does not license synonym cycling. Transducer, corpus, onset, threshold, abstraction, dissolution, and every other term with a technical or theoretical definition keep one name throughout. Variety comes from verbs, connectives, modifiers, and sentence architecture. A concept keeps its name.

**Recycling.** No non-technical content word more than three times in a paragraph. If "decision" is doing the work four times, one of those sentences should be about something else.

The linter's structural notes check the measurable parts: opener-repeat, opener-dominance, flat-rhythm, word-recycling. Terms of art are listed in `TERMS_OF_ART` in `prose-lint.py`; add to that list instead of varying a term.

## 2026-09-29: Sonic fiction as a scoped register

Per Darren: the dissertation is itself an act of sonic fiction in many of its passages. Eshun's register, in which technology and sound act with kinetic force, belongs where the dissertation is doing fiction: the speculative frame of the cryosleep question, the imagining of the tune among the Buffalo Soldier regiments, the Sun Ra material. In those passages, figurative agency and force verbs are available because the fiction is announced by its frame.

Where a passage reports what an instrument did, what a source says, or what I decided, the literal rules hold: operators named, no false agency, no force verbs. The two registers can meet on one page. They should not blur inside one sentence, because a reader has to know whether the hydrophone "forced" something in the fiction or in the lab. The CCMSAI paper stays in the literal register throughout; a proceedings paper about a prototype reads hyperbole as overclaiming.
